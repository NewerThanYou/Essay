Curiosity is Growth.
Famous scientist Albert
Einstein once said “The
important thing is not to stop
questioning. Curiosity has its
own reason for existence.” So
why is curiosity, this elusive
force, such a phenomenal
thing? Because it helps the
curious person. Curiosity helps
people learn how to question,
and thus, help themselves.
Imagine this scenario:
You, the high-schooler, have just finished your chemistry final.
You painstakingly drag yourself to check the grade, and realize
something: You did horrible. 74. For a while, you wallow in despair. You
ask yourself, “How did I let this happen?”. You tell yourself something
will change. Then the next time you do a test, the exact same thing
happens.
So why does this happen? Some people believe it’s because they
have a lower IQ, but actually, it’s mainly because they didn’t have
enough curiosity to determine their failure.
Let’s run this same scenario, but instead of a short affirmation
you’ll do better, you become curious about what exactly you did wrong.
After looking at the screen, you became curious: “What did I do
wrong?” You repeat to yourself. Soon, afterwards, you determine your
exact faults: You struggle at the visualization of electron configurations
in your head. Then afterwards, you realize something else: This means
you struggle with visualization. Knowing this, you practice your
visualization skills, and ace the next test!
One thing you can notice is that this process takes much longer,
and is more strenuous, but the result is much better.
These scenarios overall paint a contrast with a significant effect:
Curiosity gives you the power to change.
Curiosity Helps You Learn.
Studying is often simplified when
it’s talked about. When asked the best
ways to study, common hallmarks like
note-taking and repetition are repeated.
However, there’s much more to studying
than that. Quantum physicist and
Princeton alumni Richard Feynman once
said “Curiosity demands that we ask
questions, that we try to put things
together and try to understand this
multitude of aspects as perhaps resulting
from the action of a relatively small
number of elemental things and forces acting in an infinite variety of
combinations.” Richard Feynman, alongside his tremendous
accomplishments in physics, pioneered the Feynman studying method,
which emphasizes the importance of being able to summarize your
thoughts and be curious enough to question the faults in your summaries.
Curiosity and Innovation.
Steve Jobs, old CEO for Apple,
once said “Much of what I stumbled into
by following my curiosity and intuition
turned out to be priceless later on.” This
quote is priceless advice! Overall, what
curiosity culminates into is innovation.
Innovation stems directly from
asking questions and answering them in
out-of-the-box ways. Curiosity directly
helps make those questions. Steve
clearly used curiosity in many different
ways in his life, and there are plenty of
ways you can use it in yours.
You can use creativity’s ability to create innovation in many ways
for your life too. For example, you could take some time to meditate on
that problem you’ve been facing, and ask yourself questions then make
answers for it.
A Final Look at Curiosity.
If people lacked curiosity, we wouldn’t have Einstein’s fantastic
general relativity theory, Feynman’s diagrams, or Apple’s fantastic
Iphones.
2
Ultimately, curiosity’s abilities to help our growth, our
understanding of certain fields, and spark innovation shouldn’t be
underestimated just because IQ or knowledge tends to be more discussed.
So use creativity in your life, maybe you’ll be the next big thing.
